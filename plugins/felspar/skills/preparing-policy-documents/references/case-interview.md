# The case interview: when the rules live in someone's head

Some people make the right call every day but can't say the rule. Ask "when do you approve a replacement?" and you get "it depends" or "you just know." That isn't a missing rule; it's knowledge that only comes out through real cases. So start from cases, not questions about rules: the person explains decisions they've already made, you propose the rules those decisions imply, and they correct you.

## When to use it

Use the case interview instead of the rule-by-rule interview when:

- nothing is written, and the person can't state the rule when asked;
- answers to rule questions keep coming back as "it depends", "case by case" or a story about one customer;
- the person says the knowledge is "in my head", "in Sam's head" or "you'd have to see it";
- they offer to walk you through how they handle things, or paste notes or a transcript of doing so.

If the person can state the rules and just hasn't written them, use the normal interview. You can switch at any point: one "it depends" is enough to ask for the most recent case instead.

## 1. Agree the decision

Same as always: what is being decided, for what kind of case, with which possible outcomes, and who decides today. The cases have to be about this one decision. If the person decides these cases today, ask once whether they also own the policy or who does (a role). It doesn't hold up the interview: their confirmed rules are drafted either way, and marked for the owner if it's someone else.

## 2. Collect real cases

Ask for 10 to 15 recent cases this person decided. Ask for a mix, because routine cases alone teach only the obvious rule:

- **Routine ones** that went the usual way.
- **Near misses**: ones that almost went the other way.
- **Overrides**: ones where they changed someone else's call, or someone changed theirs.
- **Hand-offs**: ones they sent to someone else, and why.
- **Unsure ones**: ones they'd still argue about.

Take them however they come: typed one by one, dictated, or a pasted transcript or notes of the person talking through their week. If they type, ask for one case at a time. If they paste a batch, work through it in order.

Leave out names, account numbers and other details that identify someone. You need what the case teaches, not who it was about.

## 3. Ask three things about each case

Skip any the walkthrough already answered.

1. **What did you decide?** In their own words for the outcome.
2. **What made you decide that?** What did they look at, and which source did they trust when two disagreed?
3. **What would have changed your answer?** This is the question that finds the threshold. "If the unit were three months older?" "If it were the customer's second claim?" Push gently for a number or a condition.

Listen for:

- **Numbers and dates.** "About two weeks" gets a follow-up: "Is that 14 days? Does day 14 count?"
- **Their category words.** Keep their vocabulary for the categories and outcomes; don't rename them.
- **"Unless", "except when", "I'd check with".** Each is an exception or an escalation.
- **The source that decides.** "The form says one thing but I always go by the technician's note" is a rule about evidence.
- **Two things that changed at once.** When two cases went different ways and differ in two ways (a new vendor *and* a bigger amount), ask which one decided it: "If a long-time vendor had sent the bigger one, would you have held it?"
- **Vague words that aren't about time.** "Urgent", "large", "trusted", "established": ask what counts and who decides it, the same way you ask for days instead of "soon".
- **Who else.** "Priya would have approved that" means two people may decide differently: a decision for the owner, not a rule for you to pick.

## 4. Propose the rules, then let them correct you

After five or so cases, or as soon as a batch of five or more arrives, write the rules and definitions that would explain what they did, in their words. Show one table and ask them to answer by exception:

| Proposed rule | Cases it explains | I'd suggest |
|---|---|---|
| Units bought within 365 days get a replacement | 1, 2, 4, 7 | Keep: day 365 counts? |
| Water damage is never covered, whatever the age | 3, 6 | Keep |
| A second claim on the same unit goes to a service lead | 5 | Based on one case: confirm |

Then list, under the table, **the cases these rules don't explain**, each as one question: "Case 8: a 400-day-old unit got a replacement. What made that one different?" A case the rules can't explain is never dropped or forced to fit. It's either a missing rule, an exception, or a past call that went against what they'd do now (mark that one an expected exception and say so).

Flag any rule that rests on a single case. Never round or convert a number they stated. When the cases only bracket a threshold (held at $3,100, paid at $890), say the range and ask for the number; don't suggest one the person never said. A proposed rule is not a rule until the person says yes.

This reply is the one exception to asking one thing at a time: the table and the list of unexplained cases go together. End it with the single question that would settle the most cases, usually the unexplained case that splits the most rules. If you have fewer than about ten cases, ask for the awkward kinds still missing (a near miss, an override, a hand-off) in one line after it.

## 5. Test the rules on the rest

Run the remaining cases against the confirmed rules, in your head, and show only the ones they'd decide differently from what actually happened. Each is the next question. Stop when every case is explained, marked as an exception, or written down as an open decision for a named owner.

## 6. Fill the policy, then the stress pass

Use the confirmed rules to fill the [policy template](policy-template.md): purpose, what a case is, definitions with includes, doesn't include and examples (the cases are good examples once anonymized), rules in order, thresholds, exceptions and what happens when it's unclear. Then run [the stress pass](stress-questions.md), but only on the traps the cases didn't already settle. Say which ones the cases covered.

## 7. Keep the cases

Every confirmed case is a real decision with a known outcome. Hand them to `writing-pack-test-cases` as **confirmed cases**: each one paraphrased with made-up names and numbers, with the outcome the person confirmed. They become the start of the job's eval set.

## What not to do

- **Don't open with "what's the rule?"** to someone who already told you they can't say it. Ask for the last case they handled.
- **Don't write a rule they didn't confirm,** even when five cases point to it. Propose it; they decide.
- **Don't treat one person's habit as company policy** if they aren't the owner. Draft it, and mark it for the owner by role.
- **Don't copy a case into the document as it happened.** Examples in the policy are anonymized and reworded.
- **Don't watch or search their tools** to find cases. Cases come from the person.
